# Cover letter: Management Science

Dear Editor,

I am pleased to submit my manuscript, "Investing in Artificial General Intelligence," for consideration at *Management Science*. The abstract follows.

> Frontier AI laboratories must decide when to build irreversible compute capacity, how much, and how to split it between inference, which earns revenue now, and training, which pays off only if Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) arrives. I develop a real-options model with regime-switching demand, a duopoly entry game, and endogenous default. The optimal training fraction has a closed form that rises with the switch's arrival rate and does not depend on scale. At baseline a single firm that can choose its scale when the switch arrives waits for it, while in the duopoly one firm invests before the switch, because waiting leads to a post-switch race that competes away the rent from leading; the equilibrium is verified numerically, not shown unique. Operating costs create a shutdown boundary at which a firm stops operating without debt, and when it trains enough, optimism about the switch lowers it ("faith-based survival"). A mistaken belief costs value mainly through the training allocation. For a single firm with precommitted post-switch scale, believing the arrival rate is 0.02 when it is 0.10 costs 32% of value, against 1.3% for an optimistic error of the same size and 13% for one of the same proportion. Errors matched in expected years to the switch cost about the same, and the latter optimist is eleven times as likely as an informed firm to reach its shutdown boundary within five years. A stylized calibration maps training allocations assigned to four AI lab archetypes into implied beliefs and their consequences.

The paper develops a real-options model in which a frontier AI laboratory decides when to invest, how much compute capacity to install, and how to split it between inference, which earns revenue now, and training, which pays off only after a switch to a high-demand regime. The model is solved in its primitives: the training fraction and the post-switch policy are in closed form, and the pre-switch policy and the duopoly equilibrium are computed numerically. It makes three contributions. First, competition, not the single firm's own option, brings investment forward of the switch. At baseline a single firm that can choose its scale when the switch arrives waits for it, while in the duopoly one firm invests before the switch, because a firm that waits enters a post-switch race in which the rent from leading is competed away. At the date of first investment following is the better role, a second-mover advantage, and first investment comes at a demand level 64% lower than for a single firm whose post-switch scale is precommitted. The equilibrium is verified numerically on a grid; uniqueness is not shown, the symmetric equilibrium in mixed strategies is not solved, and the assignment of roles is a selection. The optimal training fraction rises with the arrival rate of the switch and does not depend on scale. Second, "faith-based survival": operating costs create a shutdown boundary even without debt, and when the firm trains enough, optimism about the switch lowers it, with the threshold on the training fraction in closed form for a single-boundary reduction of the default problem. Third, "Dario's dilemma": a mistaken belief about the arrival rate costs value mainly through the training allocation, and which error costs more depends on how errors are matched. With precommitted post-switch scale, believing the rate is 0.02 when it is 0.10 costs 32% of value, against 1.3% for an optimistic error of the same size and 13% for one of the same proportion (a belief of 0.50); errors matched in expected years to the switch cost about the same, and the firm that believes 0.50 is about eleven times as likely as an informed firm to reach its shutdown boundary within five years. A stylized calibration maps the training allocations assigned to four AI lab archetypes into implied beliefs and their consequences; because the allocations are assigned, it is not evidence that beliefs explain differences across firms.

I believe *Management Science* is the natural home for this work. The paper sits at the interface of finance and operations that the journal has long cultivated: it embeds a capacity-allocation problem, how to divide irreversible compute capacity between revenue-generating inference and capability-building training, inside a dynamic corporate finance model with strategic competition and endogenous default, building directly on the real-options and strategic capacity-investment tradition the journal's audience has shaped. The economics of AI infrastructure investment should interest readers across the finance, operations, and strategy areas; I would suggest the Finance department as the most natural fit for evaluation.

A detailed disclosure statement accompanies this submission.

I am mindful that refereeing a theory-heavy paper is time-consuming. To ease the verification burden, the submission includes a Lean 4 and Mathlib proof package that machine-checks parts of the algebra and single-variable calculus that follow once the stopping problems are set up, each as an identity or inequality under stated hypotheses: the characteristic roots and their ordering; the training fraction as the unique interior root of its first-order condition, and its comparative statics; the post-switch trigger, the closed-form post-switch capacity, its positivity and its optimality in the reduced capacity objective; the equivalence of smooth pasting with the closed-form default boundary, the monotonicity of that boundary in leverage and in rival capacity, and the faith-based survival threshold with its comparative statics; the properties of the installed value in the arrival rate in Corollary 1, which is stated without the option to stop; and, for the duopoly, the contest algebra behind the critical-point statement on the allocation and the fact that the follower's capacity condition has at most one root. The optimality of the stopping rules, the pre-switch two-region system as a whole, the duopoly equilibrium and every number in the paper are not formalized; the Internet Appendix states which of these are proved on paper and which are numerical. Every theorem is checked by the Lean kernel with no unproven placeholders and only the standard axioms; the package documents exactly what is and is not formalized, and the Internet Appendix records which results are analytical, conditional, or numerical. The numerical results are reproducible from the public repository, and the package includes the cached numerical solutions behind the duopoly exhibits (the duopoly version of Dario's dilemma included) and the robustness sweeps. I hope this lets the referee spend less time on the algebra and more on the economics.
